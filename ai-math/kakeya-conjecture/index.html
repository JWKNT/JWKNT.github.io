The Kakeya conjecture in every dimension

A Kakeya set contains a unit segment in every direction. Such a set can have zero volume. OpenAI reports a proof that it still has full dimension, in every dimension. The result follows from a stronger statement about waves.

The question

In 1917, Sōichi Kakeya asked for the smallest region in which a needle of length 1 can turn around completely. The needle has no width. It can slide and rotate in the region, and it must finish in the reverse position.

A later form of the question drops the motion. A Kakeya set in n-dimensional space contains a segment of length 1 in every direction. The needle does not need to move from one position to the next. The question is how small a Kakeya set can be.

Examples

In each shape above, the needle can turn around, and the lines show some of its positions. The disk has diameter 1 and area π/4≈0.785. The equilateral triangle has height 1 and area 1/3≈0.577. Gyula Pál showed that no convex region is smaller. The three-pointed deltoid has area π/8≈0.393, and the needle touches its boundary at both ends.

Non-convex regions can be much smaller. Abram Besicovitch showed in 1928 that the needle can turn around in a region of any positive area, however small. He also showed in 1920 that a Kakeya set in the plane can have area zero.

So area cannot measure these sets. A set can have area zero and still hold a segment in every direction. The useful measure is dimension.

Dimension by counting boxes

Cover a set with boxes of side δ and count the boxes. A segment of length 1 needs about 1/δ boxes. A filled unit square needs about 1/δ2 boxes. A filled unit cube needs about 1/δ3 boxes. The exponent is the dimension.

The exponent does not have to be a whole number. To make the Cantor set, remove the middle third of a segment, then the middle third of each piece, and continue without end. Its total length is zero. It needs 2k boxes of side 3−k, so its dimension is log⁡2/log⁡3≈0.631.

The exact statement uses Hausdorff dimension, a refined form of this count that also handles sets with very irregular structure. The Cantor set shows that zero size and positive dimension can occur together.

The Kakeya conjecture says that every Kakeya set in n-dimensional space has dimension n. Thus a set can have zero volume, but it cannot be thin at every scale. Roy Davies proved the case n=2 in 1971. Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl posted a proof of the case n=3 in 2025. OpenAI's release of 6 October 2026 included a theorem for n=4.

The same question with tubes

Thicken each segment to a tube of width δ. In n dimensions there are about (1/δ)n−1 clearly different directions, and each tube has volume about δn−1. So the volumes of the tubes add up to about 1.

Take n=3 and δ=1/100. There are about 10,000 tubes, and each has volume about 1/10000. If the tubes did not overlap, their union would have volume about 1. Besicovitch's result shows that overlap can make the union smaller.

The Kakeya maximal conjecture limits this overlap. It implies that the union has volume at least cδε, for each ε>0, with a constant c that depends on ε. So the volume can fall as δ falls, but only more slowly than any power of δ.

The result

The OpenAI paper states this theorem for every dimension n≥2:

Every subset of ℝn with a unit segment in each direction has Hausdorff dimension n.

The paper also proves the Kakeya maximal conjecture. Both statements follow from a stronger theorem about waves, the local smoothing conjecture of Christopher Sogge from 1991.

A wave can focus and become very large at one instant. Local smoothing says that this extreme focus is short-lived. If you average over a short time, the wave is smaller than its worst instant, by an exact amount. The connection is geometric. Short pieces of a wave travel along thin tubes, so a limit on the focus of waves gives a limit on the overlap of tubes.

What the result does not say

  • It does not give a minimum volume. Kakeya sets of volume zero exist in every dimension n≥2. The result concerns dimension.
  • It does not change the needle answer. The needle can still turn around in a region of any positive area.
  • It does not explain the proof. OpenAI released raw AI output, and it says that researchers must still explain the argument.

Why the result matters

The conjecture looks like a puzzle about needles, but it sits at the center of a chain of statements in analysis. Local smoothing implies the Kakeya statements. The same paper deduces these other results:

  • The Bochner–Riesz conjecture, which concerns how a function can be rebuilt from its frequencies.
  • Fourier restriction estimates, which concern functions with frequencies on a sphere or similar surface.
  • Sharp bounds for Stein's square function and for local smoothing of the Schrödinger equation.

Before 2026, local smoothing was solved only for n=2, in 2019 by Larry Guth, Hong Wang and Ruixiang Zhang. Wang won a Fields Medal in 2026, and her Kakeya work with Zahl is a large part of the record.

How far the result has been checked
