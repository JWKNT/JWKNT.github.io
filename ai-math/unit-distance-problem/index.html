Erdős's unit distance problem

This problem asks for the largest number of pairs at distance exactly 1 among n points in the plane. Paul Erdős expected barely more than n. An OpenAI model disproved this in May 2026. A later OpenAI manuscript also lowers the long-standing upper limit. The true growth rate remains unknown.

The question

Place n points in the plane. Count the pairs of points that have distance exactly 1. Write u(n) for the largest count that any arrangement of n points can reach. Erdős asked in 1946 how fast u(n) grows.

The value 1 is only a choice of unit. Any arrangement can be scaled. So the question asks how many times one distance can repeat among n points.

For large n, start with a square grid of spacing 1. Each point has 4 neighbors at distance 1, so there are about 2n pairs. Erdős saw that a different spacing gives more.

Use spacing 1/5. Two grid points now have distance 1 when their offset (p,q) in grid steps satisfies p2+q2=25. This has 12 solutions: (±5,0), (0,±5), (±3,±4) and (±4,±3). So each point far from the edge has 12 neighbors.

Spacing 1/m gives one neighbor for each integer solution of p2+q2=m. Each new prime of the form 4k+1 in m doubles the count of solutions. This table gives exact counts for a grid of 1000 by 1000 points.

The best choice of m gives a grid with n1+c/log⁡log⁡n pairs, for a constant c. The extra exponent c/log⁡log⁡n falls to zero as n grows, very slowly. Erdős conjectured that nothing does much better. His conjecture says that u(n) grows more slowly than n1+δ, for each fixed δ>0.

In the other direction, Joel Spencer, Endre Szemerédi and William Trotter proved in 1984 that u(n) is at most a constant times n4/3. For about 40 years, nobody lowered this exponent. So the true exponent lay somewhere between 1 and 4/3.

The two results

Lower bound, May 2026. An internal OpenAI model disproved Erdős's conjecture. For infinitely many n, it constructs n points with at least n1+δ pairs at distance 1, for a fixed δ>0. The original proof states no value of δ. Will Sawin reached 0.014, and Eric Naslund, in AI-assisted work, later reached 0.043.

The construction changes the number system behind the grid. A grid point can be a Gaussian integer a+bi. In this system, 5=(2+i)(2−i), and the number (3+4i)/5 has absolute value 1. The new proof uses much larger number systems from algebraic number theory, with far more elements of this kind.

Upper bound, October 2026. An OpenAI manuscript states that the exponent 4/3 is not the limit. There are constants C and β, with β<4/3, such that every n satisfies this bound:

u(n)≤Cnβ

The manuscript does not give a numerical value for β.

What the result does not say

  • It does not find the true exponent. A wide gap remains between 1.043 and nearly 4/3.
  • It does not describe small arrangements. The new exponent matters only for enormous n. A rough calculation for this page shows that the grids above stay above n1.043 until n has about six million digits.
  • The lower bound is stated only for some n. The published constructions apply along an unbounded sequence of sizes, not to each n.
  • It does not reach the upper limit by this method. Sawin also showed that this type of construction cannot pass an exponent of about 1.2143.

Why the result matters

This problem was among the best known in combinatorial geometry, and Erdős offered a prize for it. The prevailing belief was that the grid is nearly the best arrangement. OpenAI describes the result as the first time that AI autonomously solved a prominent open problem at the center of a subfield.

The method also matters. The proof connects a simple question about points to deep tools from algebraic number theory, such as class field towers. Thomas Bloom wrote in the companion paper that number theory has more to say about these questions than people had suspected.

How far the result has been checked

A group of outside mathematicians checked the lower bound. A companion paper explains it. Its authors are Noga Alon, Thomas Bloom, Timothy Gowers, Daniel Litt, Will Sawin, Arul Shankar, Jacob Tsimerman, Victor Wang and Melanie Matchett Wood.

The upper bound comes with a Lean proof of the exact statement above in OpenAI's public repository. The manuscript is raw AI output from the release of 6 October 2026. A search on 5 November 2026 found no published human check of this upper bound.
