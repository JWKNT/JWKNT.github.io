Unit Distance Problem

Short version

Put n dots on a sheet of paper. Measure the distance between each pair of dots, and count the pairs that are exactly 1 apart. The question is how large that count can be. Write u(n) for the largest possible count.

Small cases show the idea. Three dots in an equilateral triangle give 3 pairs. Four dots, as two triangles that share a side, give 5 pairs. Seven dots, as a hexagon with its center, give 12 pairs. Exact answers are known only for small n.

For many dots, start with a square grid with spacing 1. Each dot has 4 neighbors at distance 1: left, right, up and down. Each pair has two dots, so the count is about 2n.

Now make the spacing smaller. With spacing 1/5, a dot that is 3 steps across and 4 steps up is exactly 1 away. This is the 3-4-5 triangle: (3/5)2+(4/5)2=1. Each dot now has 12 neighbors at distance 1, as the figure shows.

Other spacings give even more. Spacing 1/65 gives 16 neighbors, and spacing 1/1105 gives 32. Paul Erdős showed that the best grids give about n1+small pairs, where the small part slowly shrinks to 0 as n grows.

Erdős conjectured that nothing does much better than these grids. A conjecture is a statement that nobody has proved yet. In this one, the raised number on n, called the exponent, gets closer and closer to 1 as n grows. Mathematicians had also proved that the count is at most a constant times n4/3. So the true exponent lay somewhere between 1 and 4/3.

The exponent makes a large difference for large n. For one million dots, n4/3 is 100 million pairs, which is 100 per dot. A grid with a well-chosen spacing gives about 26 per dot.

An OpenAI model disproved the conjecture. For infinitely many n, it builds an arrangement with at least n1+δ pairs, for a fixed number δ above 0. Later work reached δ=0.043, so the exponent is at least 1.043 for those n.

Here is the idea behind it. Treat each grid point as a number a+bi, where i is the square root of −1. Steps of length 1 then come from numbers such as (3+4i)/5. The new construction uses much larger number systems, which have far more numbers of this kind.

A later OpenAI manuscript reports that the upper exponent is also below 4/3, by an unstated amount. The exact exponent is still unknown.
