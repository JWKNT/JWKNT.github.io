Navier–Stokes blow-up

The Navier–Stokes equations describe how fluids move. OpenAI reports a proof that a smooth force on a smooth fluid at rest can produce infinite speed in finite time. The case with no force remains open, and aircraft and weather models do not change.

The question

The Navier–Stokes equations apply Newton's second law, force equals mass times acceleration, to each small parcel of a fluid. Engineers use them for aircraft, weather and blood flow. They treat a fluid as a continuous material and not as separate molecules.

In three dimensions, for a fluid of constant density, the equations have this form:

∂u∂t+(u⋅∇)u=−∇p+νΔu+f∇⋅u=0
TermMeaning
uThe velocity of the fluid at each point and time
∂u/∂t+(u⋅∇)uThe acceleration of a parcel. The fluid carries its own velocity along, so this term contains u twice
−∇pThe force from differences in pressure
νΔuViscosity, with strength ν>0. It smooths differences in velocity
fAn external force, such as a stirring force
∇⋅u=0The fluid does not compress

A function is smooth if all its derivatives exist. The question is whether a solution that starts smooth stays smooth for all time. It can fail if the speed grows without limit in finite time. This event is a singularity, or blow-up.

Examples

A simple equation can blow up, even though it contains nothing infinite. Compare two equations that start at y(0)=1.

The square makes the difference. As y grows, its rate of growth grows faster. The term (u⋅∇)u in the Navier–Stokes equations also contains the unknown twice.

A damping term can stop this growth, but only for small values. The equation dy/dt=y2−y is a simple model of this effect. If y(0)=1/2, then y=1/(1+et) falls toward zero. If y(0)=2, then y=1/(1−et/2) blows up at t=ln⁡2≈0.693. Viscosity has a similar role in the fluid equations. It is known that small smooth initial velocities stay smooth.

These equations are simple models. They show what blow-up means. They do not show that the fluid equations behave in the same way.

The Millennium Prize problem

The Clay Mathematics Institute named this question one of seven Millennium Prize Problems in 2000. The official statement by Charles Fefferman gives four alternatives, and it asks for a proof of one of them.

StatementSpaceForceClaim
AAll of spaceNoneEach smooth start stays smooth
BA periodic boxNoneEach smooth start stays smooth
CAll of spaceSmooth force permittedSome smooth start and force break down
DA periodic boxSmooth force permittedSome smooth start and force break down

The result

OpenAI reports proofs of statements C and D. For every viscosity ν>0, there are a smooth start and a smooth force with no smooth solution for all time. In all of space, the statement also requires bounded energy.

In the construction, the fluid starts at rest. A smooth force acts on it, and the energy stays finite. A vortex spirals inward and stretches. Its center shrinks and speeds up, until the speed has no limit at a finite time.

The force itself stays smooth. So the infinite speed comes from the motion of the fluid, and not from an infinite force. Communications Physics notes that the force is tuned at each moment to feed the shrinking vortex.

What the result does not say

  • It does not settle a fluid with no force. Statements A and B concern that case. Communications Physics wrote on 14 October 2026 that the case is still unknown, and many mathematicians regard it as the main question.
  • It does not change aircraft or weather models. This is one special solution, built with a designed force. It says little about typical flows, and the equations remain well tested.
  • It does not say that real water reaches infinite speed. Real fluids are made of molecules. George Karniadakis told Nature that, in air, the singularity appears at a vortex about 70 nanometers wide, where the continuous model no longer applies.
  • It does not mean that the prize has been awarded. OpenAI says that it does not intend to claim the prize.

Why the result matters

Jean Leray proved in 1934 that weak solutions exist. Whether they stay smooth remained open for about 90 years. The new result shows that the equations with a smooth force do not guarantee a smooth solution.

A related OpenAI paper reports a singularity for the Euler equations, which have no viscosity, with no external force. So the two reports together cover a fluid with no viscosity and no force, and a fluid with viscosity and a smooth force. A fluid with viscosity and no force remains open.

How far the result has been checked

OpenAI states that about 10,000 AI agents reached the result in about 88 hours. It states that GPT-6 Astra then produced the Lean proof in 17 more hours. The Lean repository states the results for all of space and for a periodic box.

On 11 September 2026, the Clay Mathematics Institute wrote that the problem has "apparently been settled". It also wrote that its process is deliberately unhurried, and it has announced no prize decision. Communications Physics wrote that experts believe that the proof is correct, but that nobody yet understands it well.

The credit history is disputed. Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge posted related results on the Euler equations with a force just before the announcement. OpenAI recognizes their priority on that case. Buckmaster says that OpenAI followed their route after it learned of their work. OpenAI says that a rumor prompted its effort, but it denies that their work influenced its system. All of these results use methods from Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa.
