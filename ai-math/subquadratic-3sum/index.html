Subquadratic 3SUM

3SUM asks if three of n given integers sum to zero. In {−7,−3,2,5,8,11} the answer is yes, because −7+2+5=0.

The textbook method sorts the numbers. Then, for each number, it scans the sorted list from both ends for a matching pair. This takes about n2 steps, and decades of work removed only logarithmic factors.

The 3SUM hypothesis says that this is essentially optimal: no algorithm takes n2−ε steps, for any fixed ε>0. A similar hypothesis says the same about n3 for all-pairs shortest paths. Researchers used these hypotheses to explain why many other problems resist faster algorithms.

Josh Alman and Virginia Vassilevska Williams report that both are false. Their exponents are in the table, for integers of polynomial size. Claude discovered the algorithm, and the authors then simplified and extended it.

Everything comes from one new method. It computes selected entries of a product of two thin matrices faster than it could compute those entries one by one. Known reductions then carry the gain to 3SUM and the other problems.

The authors say that the hidden constants are enormous, so the algorithms are potentially impractical. The change is in the theory: n2 is not a barrier for 3SUM.
