ℤ is Diophantine in ℚ

ℤ is the set of integers, and ℚ is the set of fractions. A set S of fractions is Diophantine in ℚ if one polynomial P with integer coefficients can test membership in S:

q∈S⟺P(q,w1,…,wr)=0for some fractions w1,…,wr

The extra unknowns only have to exist. This can express more than it first appears to. The nonzero fractions are Diophantine, because q≠0 exactly when qw=1 has a solution. The fractions q≥0 are Diophantine, because a fraction is non-negative exactly when it is a sum of four squares of fractions.

The statement says that "is an integer" is also such a condition. One equation can separate 3 from 7/2, although a polynomial can only add and multiply and cannot read a denominator.

This looks unlikely. The solutions of an equation form a shape, and S is the projection of its rational points onto the q axis. Rational points usually spread densely along a shape, so the projection has no gaps. The integers have gaps everywhere. A conjecture of Barry Mazur would have ruled out such an equation.

A new manuscript reports a construction of P. It has about 293 billion variables, and its degree is below a tower of seven 2s. Only its existence matters. With it, every question about integer solutions becomes a question about rational solutions, which connects to Hilbert's tenth problem.
