Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis

The Riemann zeta function starts as this sum, and a standard method extends it to complex s:

ζ(s)=1+12s+13s+⋯

Apart from easy zeros at −2,−4,−6,…, all its zeros lie in the strip 0<Re⁡s<1, where Re⁡s is the real part of s.

These zeros control the primes. Let π(x) count the primes up to x. The best simple estimate is li⁡(x)=∫0xdt/ln⁡t. Riemann's formula writes the error as a sum over the zeros, and a zero with real part β contributes about xβ. So the rightmost zero sets the size of the error.

The Riemann hypothesis says that every zero in the strip has real part 1/2. The error is then about x. The quasi-Riemann hypothesis asks for less: some fixed β<1 has no zeros to its right. Before this result, zeros arbitrarily near the line Re⁡s=1 could not be excluded.

OpenAI reports a proof with β=7/8. The error in the prime count is then below a constant times xp, for every p>7/8. Earlier bounds saved less than any fixed power of x. The same limit holds for Dirichlet L-functions, which control primes in remainder classes, such as primes that end in 7. The Riemann hypothesis itself stays open.
