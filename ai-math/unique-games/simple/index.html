Unique Games Conjecture

Short version

Start with arithmetic on a small clock. With 5 values, the numbers are 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4, and after 4 you return to 0. So 3+4=2 on this clock. This is called arithmetic modulo 5.

A unique game is a list of rules about some unknown values on such a clock. Each rule links two unknowns, for example: b equals a plus 2. If you know a, the rule gives exactly one value for b. That is the meaning of "unique". The word "game" comes from an older form of the problem with two players.

If all the rules can hold together, a solution is easy to find. Choose a value for a, follow the rules, and fill in everything else. In the figure, a=0 gives b=2, then c=3, d=2 and e=0, and all eight rules hold.

Now suppose that only 99% of the rules can hold, and you do not know which 1% are bad. Following the rules now fails. A chain of 100 rules hits a bad rule with probability about 63%. After that point, every value is wrong.

So you might aim much lower and try to satisfy only 1% of the rules. That sounds easy. A conjecture is a statement that nobody has proved yet. The Unique Games Conjecture says that it is still hard, if the clock is large enough. When 99% of the rules can hold, finding values that satisfy even 1% of them is NP-hard.

NP-hard means that a fast method for this task would also solve 3-SAT and every other problem in a large family called NP. Nobody has a fast method for those problems, and most researchers expect that none exists. "Fast" here means that the work grows like a fixed power of the input size.

An OpenAI manuscript reports a proof. It gives a fast way to turn any 3-SAT puzzle into a unique game. If the puzzle has a solution, nearly all the rules can hold. If not, almost none can. So a fast way to tell these two kinds of game apart would solve 3-SAT.

The conjecture is famous because of what it implies. Many problems have a fast method that gives a good but not perfect answer. The conjecture says that many of those methods are already the best possible, unless every problem in NP has a fast method.

Here is one example. Split the people at a party into two groups, so that as many pairs of rivals as possible end up in different groups. A known method guarantees at least 87.8% of the best possible count. If the proof is right, no fast method can guarantee more, unless every problem in NP has a fast method.
