A Hadamard matrix is a square matrix of \(+1\) and \(-1\) whose rows are pairwise orthogonal. So any two rows agree in exactly half of their positions:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} + & + & + & + \\ + & - & + & - \\ + & + & - & - \\ + & - & - & + \end{pmatrix} \]

The order is the number of rows. Three orthogonal rows split the columns into four groups of equal size, so the order must be 1, 2 or a multiple of 4. The Hadamard conjecture says that every multiple of 4 occurs.

A candidate is easy to check with one matrix multiplication. Finding one is hard. Order 668 has \(2^{446\,224}\) sign matrices, and known constructions cover only some orders. So 668 stood as the smallest order with no known example for about 20 years.

Alpöge, Voinov and Reynolds-Haertle, working with Claude, announced matrices for all twelve open orders below 2000. These are 668, 716, 892, 1132, 1244, 1388, 1436, 1676, 1772, 1916, 1948 and 1964. Other mathematicians verified them. The smallest unknown order is now 2396. The conjecture stays open, because these are individual matrices and not a general construction.

Separately, OpenAI reports a proof for a restricted form. If each row is a cyclic shift of the first row, the order can only be 1 or 4.
