This title uses two symbols. \(\mathbb{Z}\) means the whole numbers, such as \(-2\), \(0\), \(1\) and \(3\). \(\mathbb{Q}\) means the fractions, such as \(1/2\) and \(-3/4\). Every whole number is also a fraction, because \(3\) is the same as \(3/1\).

A polynomial is a formula that only adds and multiplies numbers and letters. For example, \(x^2 + 3xy - 5\) is a polynomial. A polynomial equation sets such a formula equal to zero.

You can use an equation as a test. You have a number \(q\), and the equation has \(q\) and some extra letters in it. The number \(q\) passes if some fractions for the extra letters make the equation true. If no fractions work, \(q\) fails.

Here is an example, with the equation \(qw - 1 = 0\). The number \(3/4\) passes, because \(w = 4/3\) gives \((3/4)(4/3) - 1 = 0\). The number \(0\) fails, because \(0\) times any \(w\) is \(0\), and \(0 - 1\) is never \(0\). So this equation tests if a number is not zero.

Here is a second example, with the equation \(q - w^2 = 0\). The number \(9/4\) passes, because \(w = 3/2\) works. The number \(2\) fails, because no fraction squared gives \(2\). So this equation tests if a number is the square of a fraction.

A set of fractions is Diophantine in \(\mathbb{Q}\) if one equation like this can test for it. The name comes from Diophantus, a Greek mathematician who studied equations. So the nonzero numbers are Diophantine in \(\mathbb{Q}\), and so are the squares.

The title says that the whole numbers are also Diophantine in \(\mathbb{Q}\). One equation exists that every whole number passes and every other fraction fails. So \(3\) and \(-12\) pass, and \(7/2\) and \(1/3\) fail.

This is surprising. You can see that \(7/2\) is not a whole number by looking at its bottom number. An equation cannot do that. It can only add and multiply.

There was also a strong reason to doubt it. The solutions of an equation form a curve or a surface, and the figure shows one. Fraction solutions usually crowd together along such a shape, so the points directly below them on the line crowd together too. But the whole numbers sit apart, with a gap of 1 between neighbors. A conjecture of Barry Mazur, a statement that nobody had proved, would have ruled out such an equation.

A new manuscript reports that the equation exists. It is enormous. It has about 293 billion letters, and its highest power is below a tower of seven 2s. In a tower, each number is the power of the number below it. A tower of four 2s is 65,536, and a tower of five has 19,729 digits. Only the existence of the equation matters here.

Here is what it gives. A question about whole numbers becomes a question about fractions. To ask for a whole number \(x\), ask for a fraction \(x\) that also passes the test. This connects to Hilbert's tenth problem, which asks if a computer can decide such questions.
