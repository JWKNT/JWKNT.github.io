The Riemann hypothesis says where the zeros of the zeta function lie, and those zeros control the errors in prime counts. OpenAI reports a weaker statement: no zero has real part above 7/8. Before this result, no fixed limit below 1 was known.

{{facts}}

The question

A prime is a whole number above 1 with no divisors except 1 and itself. Primes become less common as numbers grow. Near a large number \(x\), about one number in \(\ln x\) is prime.

Write \(\pi(x)\) for the number of primes up to \(x\). The logarithmic integral \(\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_0^x dt / \ln t\) adds up that density, and it estimates \(\pi(x)\). The question is how large the error \(\operatorname{li}(x) - \pi(x)\) can become.

In this range the error stays below \(x^{1/2}\). A table cannot show what happens for all \(x\). A proof must control the error for numbers far beyond any computation.

The zeta function and its zeros

The Riemann zeta function starts as this sum:

\[ \zeta(s) = 1 + \frac{1}{2^s} + \frac{1}{3^s} + \frac{1}{4^s} + \cdots \]

For \(s = 2\), the sum approaches \(\pi^2 / 6 \approx 1.6449\). The first 10 terms give 1.5498, and the first 1000 terms give 1.6439. For \(s = 1\), the sum grows without limit.

The sum converges only when the real part of \(s\) is above 1. A standard method extends \(\zeta\) to all complex numbers except \(s = 1\). A complex number \(s = \sigma + it\) has real part \(\sigma\), written \(\operatorname{Re} s\).

Unique factorization connects this function to the primes. Euler showed that \(\zeta\) is a product over all primes \(p\), with one factor \(1/(1 - p^{-s})\) for each prime. So the function holds information about primes in a form that analysis can use.

A zero of \(\zeta\) is a number \(s\) with \(\zeta(s) = 0\). The numbers \(-2, -4, -6, \dots\) are zeros, and they are easy to find. All other zeros lie in the strip \(0 < \operatorname{Re} s < 1\). The first of these is near \(\tfrac12 + 14.1347i\).

Riemann's formula writes the error in the prime count as a sum over these zeros. A zero with real part \(\beta\) gives a wave of size about \(x^{\beta}\). So the zero with the largest real part sets the size of the error.

The constant \(C\) in this table is unknown or large. So the table compares growth rates and not exact limits. Mathematicians have checked that the first 12 trillion zeros have real part exactly \(1/2\).

{{divider}}

The result

The OpenAI manuscript states that the zeta function has no zero in this half-plane:

\[ \operatorname{Re} s > \tfrac{7}{8} \]

A symmetry of \(\zeta\) then excludes zeros with \(0 < \operatorname{Re} s < 1/8\). So each zero in the strip has real part between \(1/8\) and \(7/8\), and the limit \(\beta = 7/8\) holds. The same half-plane has no zeros for all Dirichlet L-functions, and for a family of Hecke L-functions.

A Dirichlet L-function is a sum of the same form as \(\zeta\), with a periodic pattern of signs or roots of unity in its terms. These functions control primes in fixed remainder classes.

What the result does not say

  • It is not the Riemann hypothesis. The Riemann hypothesis puts every such zero on the line \(\operatorname{Re} s = 1/2\). That statement remains open.
  • It does not exclude the line itself. The theorem does not exclude a zero with real part exactly \(7/8\).
  • It does not predict individual primes. It limits the error in a count over a long range.
  • It does not show a path to the full hypothesis. Will Sawin and Thomas Bloom told Quanta that the full hypothesis still appears out of reach.

Why the result matters

A fixed limit gives a power saving in the error. The error is below \(C x^{p}\) for each \(p > 7/8\). The earlier bounds saved less than any fixed power of \(x\). Maksym Radziwiłł told Quanta that the implications are "staggering".

Primes in remainder classes. Primes above 5 end in 1, 3, 7 or 9, and these four classes hold nearly equal counts. Below \(10^{8}\) the counts are 1,440,298, 1,440,474, 1,440,495 and 1,440,186. The shorter manuscript gives an error below a constant times \(x^{11/12} \log x\) for all such classes with modulus up to \(x\).

Square roots modulo a prime. The squares modulo 7 are 1, 2 and 4, so 3 is the smallest non-square. The manuscript shows that the smallest non-square modulo \(p\) is at most a constant times a fixed power of \(\log p\). This proves a conjecture of Vinogradov. It also gives a deterministic fast algorithm for square roots modulo \(p\).

Landau–Siegel zeros. These are possible zeros of a Dirichlet L-function very near \(s = 1\). They were a long-standing obstacle in number theory. A half-plane with no zeros excludes all of them.

How far the result has been checked

OpenAI released two manuscripts on 6 October 2026. A 199-page manuscript dated 30 September gives the 7/8 limit, with Lean proofs for \(\zeta\), for Dirichlet L-functions and for the Hecke family. A 49-page manuscript dated 5 October gives a separate proof of the weaker limit 11/12. It has no Lean proof, and a person edited it.

Mathematicians have studied the shorter manuscript because of its length. Quanta reported on 19 October that write-ups of 7 to 13 pages circulate privately, and that these experts largely agree that the result is correct. Peter Sarnak told Quanta that he will wait for humans to check all the details. No source reports a finished journal review on this date.
