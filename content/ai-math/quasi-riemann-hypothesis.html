The Riemann zeta function starts as this sum, and a standard method extends it to complex \(s\):

\[ \zeta(s) = 1 + \frac{1}{2^s} + \frac{1}{3^s} + \cdots \]

Apart from easy zeros at \(-2, -4, -6, \dots\), all its zeros lie in the strip \(0 < \operatorname{Re} s < 1\), where \(\operatorname{Re} s\) is the real part of \(s\).

These zeros control the primes. Let \(\pi(x)\) count the primes up to \(x\). The best simple estimate is \(\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_0^x dt / \ln t\). Riemann's formula writes the error as a sum over the zeros, and a zero with real part \(\beta\) contributes about \(x^{\beta}\). So the rightmost zero sets the size of the error.

The Riemann hypothesis says that every zero in the strip has real part \(1/2\). The error is then about \(\sqrt{x}\). The quasi-Riemann hypothesis asks for less: some fixed \(\beta < 1\) has no zeros to its right. Before this result, zeros arbitrarily near the line \(\operatorname{Re} s = 1\) could not be excluded.

OpenAI reports a proof with \(\beta = 7/8\). The error in the prime count is then below a constant times \(x^{p}\), for every \(p > 7/8\). Earlier bounds saved less than any fixed power of \(x\). The same limit holds for Dirichlet L-functions, which control primes in remainder classes, such as primes that end in 7. The Riemann hypothesis itself stays open.
