Elliot Glazer and several AI models report one polynomial equation that separates the integers from all other fractions. A fraction \(q\) is an integer exactly when the equation has a rational solution with \(q\) in its first position.

{{facts}}

The question

The integers are the whole numbers \(\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\). Mathematicians write \(\mathbb{Z}\) for this set. The rational numbers are the fractions \(a/b\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are integers and \(b\) is not zero. Mathematicians write \(\mathbb{Q}\) for this set.

A polynomial can only add and multiply. It cannot read a denominator. The question is whether one polynomial equation can still separate the integers from the other fractions.

A set \(S\) of rational numbers is Diophantine in \(\mathbb{Q}\) if one polynomial \(P\) with integer coefficients gives this test for every rational number \(q\):

\[ \begin{aligned} q \in S \iff\; & P(q, w_1, \dots, w_r) = 0 \text{ has a solution} \\ & \text{with every } w_i \text{ rational.} \end{aligned} \]

The unknowns \(w_1, \dots, w_r\) are witnesses. Each \(q\) in \(S\) must have witnesses. No other \(q\) can have witnesses. The witnesses are not part of the answer. They only have to exist.

Examples

These three sets are Diophantine in \(\mathbb{Q}\). Each row gives the polynomial test, one number that passes and one number that fails.

SetTestPassesFails
Nonzero numbers\(qw - 1 = 0\)\(q = 3/4\) with \(w = 4/3\)\(q = 0\), because \(0 \cdot w - 1 = -1\) for every \(w\)
Squares\(q - w^2 = 0\)\(q = 9/4\) with \(w = 3/2\)\(q = 2\), because \(\sqrt{2}\) is not rational
Numbers \(q \ge 0\)\(q - w_1^2 - w_2^2 - w_3^2 - w_4^2 = 0\)\(q = 3/7\) with \(w = (4/7, 2/7, 1/7, 0)\)\(q = -1\), because a sum of squares is never negative

The third row uses a theorem of Lagrange: every non-negative integer is a sum of four integer squares. For a fraction \(a/b\) with \(b > 0\), write \(a/b = ab/b^2\). Then write \(ab\) as four squares and divide each square by \(b^2\). For \(q = 3/7\), the number \(ab\) is \(21 = 4^2 + 2^2 + 1^2 + 0^2\).

Several conditions can become one polynomial. For rational numbers, \(A = 0\) and \(B = 0\) both hold exactly when \(A^2 + B^2 = 0\). Thus a long list of conditions can use one equation. The equation becomes larger, but its form stays the same.

The witnesses act as extra coordinates. The solutions of \(P = 0\) form a shape in a space with \(r + 1\) dimensions. The set \(S\) is the shadow of the rational points of that shape on the \(q\) axis.

Why the integers were difficult

Rational points usually spread along a shape. On the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\), two examples are \((3/5, 4/5)\) and \((5/13, 12/13)\). Rational points come arbitrarily near every point of this circle. The shadow of such a set has no gaps.

The integers have gaps. A polynomial definition of \(\mathbb{Z}\) needs a shape whose rational points have a shadow with exactly these gaps.

Barry Mazur conjectured a limit on this behavior. For a shape defined by polynomials, he expected the closure of the rational points to have finitely many separate pieces. He observed that this conjecture would rule out a polynomial definition of \(\mathbb{Z}\). So the definition appeared unlikely.

Other kinds of formula already defined \(\mathbb{Z}\) inside \(\mathbb{Q}\). They use "for all" as well as "there exists". A Diophantine definition can use only "there exists".

The result

The manuscript states that one polynomial \(P\) with integer coefficients gives this test for every rational number \(q\):

\[ \begin{aligned} q \in \mathbb{Z} \iff\; & \text{there is } w \in \mathbb{Q}^{292\,890\,231\,904} \\ & \text{with } P(q, w) = 0. \end{aligned} \]

It also gives upper limits for the size of \(P\).

  • \(P\) has at most 292,890,231,905 variables, including \(q\).
  • The degree of \(P\) is less than \(2 \uparrow\uparrow 7\).
  • Each coefficient of \(P\) has absolute value less than \(2 \uparrow\uparrow 8\).

The symbol \(2 \uparrow\uparrow n\) means a tower of \(n\) twos, with each two as the exponent of the two below it.

TowerValue
\(2 \uparrow\uparrow 3\)\(2^{2^2} = 16\)
\(2 \uparrow\uparrow 4\)\(2^{16} = 65\,536\)
\(2 \uparrow\uparrow 5\)\(2^{65\,536}\), a number with 19,729 digits
\(2 \uparrow\uparrow 6\)A number with about \(6 \times 10^{19\,727}\) digits
\(2 \uparrow\uparrow 7\)2 to the power \(2 \uparrow\uparrow 6\). This is the limit on the degree

The observable universe has about \(10^{80}\) atoms, a number with 81 digits. A file of about 23 megabytes still describes \(P\), because it stores nested formulas and substitutions. It does not list the terms of \(P\).

What the result does not say

  • It does not give a practical test for integers. A reader can check a reduced fraction much faster than a search for witnesses.
  • It does not claim that these sizes are the smallest possible. The manuscript also says that some of the variables may have no real effect.
  • It is not the first claim that no algorithm decides rational equations. The earlier preprint on Hilbert's tenth problem over \(\mathbb{Q}\) made that claim, and this manuscript builds on it.
  • It does not come with a human-checked proof. A later section gives the current state.

Why the result matters

Integer questions become rational questions. Suppose that a question asks for integers \(x\) and \(y\) with \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\). The same question can ask for rational \(x\) and \(y\), with witnesses \(v\) and \(w\), that satisfy three equations: \(x^2 + y^2 - 25 = 0\), \(P(x, v) = 0\) and \(P(y, w) = 0\). The sum of their squares then gives one equation.

The manuscript gives three consequences.

  • Mazur's conjecture is false. The rational points of \(P = 0\) have a shadow equal to \(\mathbb{Z}\). So their closure has infinitely many separate pieces.
  • A universal polynomial exists. One fixed polynomial \(U(e, n, x)\) has a rational solution \(x\) exactly when program \(e\) stops on input \(n\).
  • Rational equations have the difficulty of the halting problem. Each of the two problems converts to the other by a computable change of input.

How far the result has been checked

Elliot Glazer posted the manuscript and a public repository on 5 November 2026, the date of this page. The repository credits Glazer working with the AI models Astra 6, Sol 6.1 and Opus 5.5. It states that Fable 5.1 audited the work.

The reviews so far are AI reviews. The repository states that these reviews are distinct from independent human peer review and machine verification. It also states that explicit internal premises remain.

The Lean formalization is incomplete. Lean is a language in which a computer checks a proof step by step. At the 4 November handoff, the main declaration still contained sorry, the Lean placeholder for a missing proof. Read the theorem on this page as a report, not as an established result.

An earlier manuscript by Sohail Farhangi with ChatGPT 6, dated 11 October 2026, gave a conditional form of the statement. It depends on four arithmetic inputs from OpenAI.
