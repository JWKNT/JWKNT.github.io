Place \(n\) points in the plane and count the pairs at distance exactly 1. Let \(u(n)\) be the largest possible count. The question is how fast \(u(n)\) grows.

A square grid does better than it first appears to. With spacing 1, each point has 4 neighbors. With spacing \(1/5\), it has 12, because \(p^2 + q^2 = 25\) has 12 integer solutions. A good choice of spacing gives \(n^{1 + c/\log\log n}\) pairs.

Erdős conjectured that this is essentially the best: for each \(\delta > 0\), \(u(n)\) stays below \(n^{1+\delta}\) for large \(n\). The best known upper limit was a constant times \(n^{4/3}\). So the true exponent lay between 1 and \(4/3\).

Two results move these limits. An OpenAI model disproved the conjecture: for infinitely many \(n\), some arrangement has at least \(n^{1+\delta}\) pairs, for a fixed \(\delta > 0\). It replaces the integers behind the grid with much larger number systems. Later work reached \(\delta = 0.043\). A later OpenAI manuscript reports that the upper exponent is also below \(4/3\), by an unstated fixed amount.

\[ n^{1.043} \lesssim u(n) \lesssim n^{\beta}, \qquad \beta < 4/3 \]

The exact exponent remains unknown.
