Color each edge between \(N\) points with one of \(k\) colors. The Ramsey number \(R_k(3)\) is the smallest \(N\) that forces a triangle with all three edges in one color. For example, \(R_2(3) = 6\): among six people, some three are all friends or all strangers.

Exact values are rare. \(R_3(3) = 17\), and \(R_4(3)\) is only known to lie between 51 and 62. For large \(k\), the best upper limit was about \(e \cdot k!\), and the best lower limit was about \(3.28^k\).

So the open question was whether \(R_k(3)\) grows like \(c^k\) or faster. Erdős offered $100 to decide if the limit of \(R_k(3)^{1/k}\) is finite.

The lower limits came from products. Take two colorings with no one-color triangle, on \(N\) points with \(k\) colors and on \(M\) points with \(\ell\) colors. They combine into such a coloring on \(NM\) points with \(k + \ell\) colors. Repeating this gives only growth like \(c^k\).

OpenAI reports a recursive construction that grows faster. For a constant \(c > 0\) and every \(k \ge 2\):

\[ R_k(3) \ge \left( \frac{c\, k^{1/3}}{\log k} \right)^{k} \]

So the limit is infinite. With the upper limit, this shows that \(R_k(3)\) has the form \(k^{\Theta(k)}\), which means that \(\log R_k(3)\) grows like \(k \log k\). A later paper extends the method to other odd cycles.
