A map \(F\) from \(\mathbb{C}^n\) to \(\mathbb{C}^n\) has a matrix of partial derivatives. Its determinant, the Jacobian determinant, is the local scale factor for volume. Where it is not zero, \(F\) can be reversed locally.

A local inverse does not give a global inverse. The last map in the table has a nonzero determinant everywhere, yet it repeats its values. The Jacobian conjecture said that polynomials behave better: if \(F\) is a polynomial map with a constant nonzero determinant, then \(F\) has a polynomial inverse. A polynomial on \(\mathbb{C}^n\) with no zeros must be constant, so this condition only says that the determinant is never zero.

The conjecture is false for \(n \ge 3\). Levent Alpöge posted this counterexample in the variables \(x, y, z\), and credited the AI model Claude Fable 5:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_1 &= (1 + xy)^3 z + y^2 (1 + xy)(4 + 3xy) \\ F_2 &= y + 3x(1 + xy)^2 z + 3xy^2(4 + 3xy) \\ F_3 &= 2x - 3x^2 y - x^3 z \end{aligned} \]

The determinant of \(F = (F_1, F_2, F_3)\) is \(-2\) at every point, yet three points have one output:

\[ \begin{aligned} F(0, 0, -\tfrac14) &= (-\tfrac14, 0, 0) \\ F(1, -\tfrac32, \tfrac{13}{2}) &= (-\tfrac14, 0, 0) \\ F(-1, \tfrac32, \tfrac{13}{2}) &= (-\tfrac14, 0, 0) \end{aligned} \]

Direct calculation confirms both facts. Extra coordinates that the map leaves unchanged give examples for each larger \(n\). The case \(n = 2\) remains open.
