The Navier–Stokes equations are Newton's second law for a fluid. Here \(u\) is velocity, \(p\) is pressure, \(\nu > 0\) is viscosity and \(f\) is an external force:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + (u \cdot \nabla) u &= -\nabla p + \nu \Delta u + f \\ \nabla \cdot u &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

The question is whether a fluid that starts smooth stays smooth, or if its speed can become infinite in finite time. This event is a blow-up.

Blow-up can occur even when nothing in an equation is infinite. The equation \(dy/dt = y^2\) with \(y(0) = 1\) has the solution \(y = 1/(1-t)\), which reaches infinity at \(t = 1\). The fluid equations have a similar squared term, \((u \cdot \nabla) u\), and viscosity acts against it.

The Clay Millennium problem accepts a proof of either outcome. Either every smooth start stays smooth with no force, or some smooth start breaks down under a smooth force.

OpenAI reports a proof of the second outcome. A fluid at rest, driven by a smooth force, forms a vortex that spirals inward and stretches. Its speed has no limit at a finite time. The force stays smooth, so the infinity comes from the fluid itself.

A fluid with no force remains open, and many mathematicians regard that as the main question. The result concerns the equations and not real water, which is made of molecules. Aircraft and weather models do not change.
